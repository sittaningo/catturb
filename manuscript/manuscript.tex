\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}}

One of the most well-documented concepts in sociology is social
category. Social Category, such as gender, race and ethnicity, divides
and stratifies human beings, finally generating social inequality in a
broader realm of society: labor market (Goldin 2014), family (Mclanahan
2004) and health (Case and Deaton 2015). Recently, scholars have paid
more attention to intersectionality, which emphasizes on consolidation
of social categories (Collins 2015).

Going beyond examining categorical and intersectional inequality, a
growing studies have begun to argue \emph{how} social categories
consolidate and shape hidden dimension of social inequality. For
example, Weisshaar, Chavez, and Hutt (2024) coined the term ``diversity
values'' in discrimination in hiring, that personnels value females as
well as Black as the exchangeable and quantifiable social categories.
Monk, Esposito, and Lee (2021) found substantial heterogeneity among
gender and racial disparity in wage by quantifying ``bodily capital.''
As such, it advances sociological understandings of social categories to
theorize a given social category can be actually conceptualize.
＜パラフレーズ＞

Contrary to the proliferation of gender and racial categories, age has
hitherto existed as a haunting vacuum in studies of social categories.
Age as social category has long-lasting attention in sociology (Parsons
1942; Linton 1942; Neugarten, Moore, and Lowe 1965; Riley, Johnson, and
Foner 1972; Jr. and Mayer 1997; Barrett 2022). But age is now collapsed
into life course approach.＜もっと掘れる＞ As Barrett (2022, 218)
argues, ``the most common approach was to examine some given process
occurring over the life course, with scant attention to other dimensions
of age, including age discrimination, ageism, or age identity.'' Barrett
(2022) foreshadows sociology of age and reconceptualizes age category as
institution, performance and identity. However, Barrett (2022) does not
consider how age category intersects with gender and
race.＜もっと掘れる＞

In this article, I argue that old-age functions as \emph{category
turbulence} to other social categories such as gender and race. Category
turbulence is defined as a categorical characteristic that nullifies
other category's ex-ante conditions, such as gender role
(\textbf{nanika?}) and orderly bodies {[}Peters.2025e3d{]}. Concretely,
category turbulence occurs through two pathways: \emph{societal}
turbulence and \emph{bodily} turbulence. \emph{Societal} turbulence
disrupts social expectations surrounding a social category. Men serves
higher human capital than women because of division of labor (Becker
1991). Women have higher risks for paid leave for their familial
obligation (\textbf{Arrow.1973?}). Old-age men and women may be viewed
as homogeneous due to ageism (Roscigno et al. 2007) and compositional
change in human capital (Autor and Dorn 2009; Sullivan 2010).
\emph{Bodily} turbulence disrupts expectation of orderly bodies. For
example, racial difference in skin tone may be in turmoil by wrinkle and
stooped posture. In old age, social inequality in gender and race is no
longer confirmed by category turbulence.

To address category turbulence, I carry out the survey expeiment in
hiring.

These results can support alternative conceptualization of age: category
turbulence.
